Review — The Shape of Complexity: topology, random walks, and statistical methods on networks

Candidate: Gabriele Cerioli · Bachelor's thesis in Physics Draft reviewed: bozza_tesi_cerioli.pdf (53 pp.) Date: 2026-08-26 Scope of review: the text was read in full and checked for internal consistency, correctness of the derivations, and consistency between text, figures and tables. Judged as a review/benchmark thesis, not as a research manuscript.

0. Overall assessment

The draft is in good shape. The theoretical chapters (2–4) are well organised, the analytical thread (adjacency → Laplacian → random walk → diffusion → percolation → Ising → cavity/non-backtracking) is coherent, and I re-derived essentially every equation in Chapters 2–4 and Appendix A and they are correct: the master equation and its Laplacian form (43), detailed balance and the stationary distribution (44)–(46), the MFPT recursion (51), the similarity transform (57), the commute-time/resistance identity (63), the Dirichlet-energy gradient flow (64)–(65), Molloy–Reed (87), the DGM mean-field temperature (102) and its derivation in A.8, the Bethe–Peierls temperature (103) and its full derivation in A.7, the non-backtracking bifurcation (110)–(113), and the reduction of (113) to (103) for k-regular graphs. That is a solid basis.

The problems are concentrated in Chapter 5 (the numerical benchmark) and in text–figure consistency. There is one methodological error there that I would consider blocking (§2.1), one physical mislabelling that runs through the whole chapter (§2.2), and a headline conclusion that the thesis' own table contradicts (§2.3). Everything else is fixable in a revision pass.

2. Substantive errors — should be fixed before submission

2.1 The Bethe–Peierls column of Tables (121) and (122) uses the wrong formula for heterogeneous networks

This is the most serious issue, because §4.3 derives the correct result and then Chapter 5 does not use it.

Both benchmark tables label the column "T_c^BP (Eq. 103)", i.e. the k-regular formula k_BT_c = 2J/ln[k/(k−2)], evaluated with k = ⟨k⟩. Reverse-engineering the numbers confirms this:

  • 2D RGG, ⟨k⟩ = 5.2 → 2/ln(5.2/3.2) = 4.12 ✓ matches the reported 4.13
  • BA, ⟨k⟩ = 6.0 → 2/ln(6/4) = 4.93 ✓ matches the reported 4.90

But Eq. (103) is valid only for a regular tree. For a heterogeneous network the thesis' own Eq. (111)/(113) applies, with ρ(B) = (⟨k²⟩−⟨k⟩)/⟨k⟩. Using the ⟨k²⟩ implied by the thesis' own mean-field column (T_c^MF = ⟨k²⟩/⟨k⟩):

Network ⟨k⟩ ⟨k²⟩ (from T_c^MF) T_c^BP reported T_c^BP from Eq. (113)
2D RGG 5.2 32.0 4.13 ≈ 5.1
Barabási–Albert 6.0 83.0 4.90 ≈ 12.8
a-Si 4.0 16.0 2.89 2.90 ✓ (near-regular, fine)
MAC 3.0 9.0 1.82 1.82 ✓

For the BA network the reported value is off by a factor ~2.6, and it inverts the qualitative message: as printed, Table (122) shows Bethe–Peierls (4.90) below mean field (13.84) and below the simulated T_c (>6.5) — which is then read as "BP fails on scale-free networks". With the correct ρ(B) the BP estimate is 12.8, i.e. close to mean field, which is the physically expected result for a locally tree-like heterogeneous graph and is exactly what §4.3 predicts. Recompute the BP column with ρ(B) (numerically from the Hashimoto matrix if you want to capture the degree correlations of the RGG/a-Si samples, which would also be worth stating), and relabel the column.

2.2 "Topological frustration" in a purely ferromagnetic model

Chapter 5 defines F_i = N_odd(i)/N_tot(i) (Eq. 120) and calls it the local topological frustration index, then interprets every result through it ("frustration suppresses T_c", "Bipartite (No Frustration)", "geometric frustration" for pyrochlore, "tetrahedral loop echoes").

All Chapter-5 simulations use J = +1, h = 0 (Eq. 119). A ferromagnet on any graph has no frustration: the all-aligned state minimises every bond energy simultaneously, odd rings included. Frustration requires antiferromagnetic or mixed-sign couplings — which the thesis itself states correctly in §2.7 ("if the interactions are antiferromagnetic, cliques generate extreme geometric frustration"). So Chapter 5 contradicts Chapter 2 and uses a term that cannot apply.

The measurement is fine and the trend is real; only the name and the mechanism are wrong. Two options: - rename F_i to something neutral — odd-ring fraction, or short-loop / non-bipartiteness index — and drop "frustration", "echoes", "disclination" as explanatory mechanisms; or - state explicitly that F_i is being used as a proxy for local loop structure (which controls the failure of the tree approximation), not as frustration in the spin-glass sense.

The second is closer to what you actually show, since the physical content is "short loops break the Bethe approximation", not "frustration lowers T_c".

2.3 The headline claim of Table (121) is contradicted by Table (121)

Under the 2D benchmark table: "Topological frustration ⟨F⟩ systematically suppresses the ferromagnetic transition temperature relative to Mean-Field and tree-like approximations."

The table's own numbers:

Topology ⟨F⟩ T_c^sim ⟨k⟩
Square 0.00 2.35 4
Kagomé 0.50 2.20 4
MAC 0.55 1.54 3
RGG 0.80 2.43 5.2

The RGG has the highest ⟨F⟩ and the highest Tc^sim. There is no monotonic relation. What the data actually show is that Tc is dominated by ⟨k⟩ — and ⟨k⟩ is not held fixed across the four systems, so the comparison is confounded. The only clean, defensible comparison in the table is Square vs Kagomé (both z = 4, 2.27 vs 2.14), and in 3D Simple Cubic vs Pyrochlore (both z = 6). Restrict the claim to the equal-coordination pairs, or add a Tc/⟨k⟩ (or Tc/T_c^MF) column so the degree effect is normalised out.

Related: the benchmark systems are introduced (p. 35) as "four representative two-dimensional topologies with comparable mean coordination (⟨k⟩ ≈ 3–4)", but the RGG has ⟨k⟩ ≈ 5.2 (p. 38). Fix one or the other.

2.4 Molloy–Reed is invoked where it does not apply and does not agree

Twice (p. 42 and Conclusions §6.2 point 2): "In agreement with the Molloy-Reed criterion (Eq. 87), since both networks share ⟨k⟩ ≈ 3.0 and ⟨k²⟩ ≈ 9.0…" — and in the Conclusions this becomes "validating the Molloy-Reed criterion".

With ⟨k⟩ = 3, ⟨k²⟩ = 9, Eq. (87) gives p_c = 3/(9−3) = 0.5. The measured thresholds are 0.653 (bond) and 0.697 (site). Molloy–Reed is a tree-like/uncorrelated result; graphene and MAC are planar, loopy, spatially embedded — precisely the regime where it fails, by ~30–40% here. Nothing is being validated.

The correct statement is the qualitative one you actually demonstrate: p_c is set by the coordination number and is insensitive to the presence of 5–7 rings, i.e. amorphisation at fixed ⟨k⟩ does not change the threshold. Keep that, cite the honeycomb thresholds properly (Sykes–Essam for 1−2sin(π/18)), and either drop Molloy–Reed or use it explicitly as a counter-example showing where the tree approximation breaks.

2.5 Text contradicts its own figure on the MAC targeted attack

Text (p. 42) and Conclusions §6.2 point 2: "removing only f ≈ 0.05 of the top-betweenness atoms instantly destroys 50% of the macroscopic cluster (P∞ → 0.5)".

Fig. 22(d): the red targeted-attack curve does drop to ≈ 0.5 at f ≈ 0.05 ✓ — but the snapshot strip underneath the same panel is labelled f = 5%, P∞ = 0.95, and the caption says the snapshots are from the targeted attack. The graphene snapshots in panel (c) are consistent with their red curve (f = 8% → 0.74 ✓, f = 18% → 0.08 ✓); the MAC ones match neither the red nor the green curve. Regenerate the MAC snapshot labels (or the snapshots) — as it stands, a reader checking the figure will conclude the headline number is wrong.

2.6 Eq. (72) — the "exact duality" is not exact on a general graph

(H_t)_ij = Σ_s (tλ)^s e^{−tλ}/s! · p_ij^(s) — "the macroscopic heat kernel is precisely the Poisson average of the discrete random walk"

Poisson subordination requires the generator to have the form L = λ(I − M). With L = D − A and M = D^{−1}A, L = D(I − M), so L = λ(I − M) only when D = λI, i.e. only on a k-regular graph. On a heterogeneous graph — the whole subject of the thesis — e^{−Lt} is not the Poisson average of M^s. Also, λ is never defined in Eq. (72), and it collides with the eigenvalue symbol used everywhere else.

Fix: either restrict the statement to k-regular graphs, or state it for the normalised Laplacian L_rw = I − M (where it is exact and standard), and say so.

2.7 The Cayley tree ≠ Bethe lattice slip (§4.3)

"In the thermodynamic limit N → ∞, the number of leaves (boundary nodes) tends to zero, and the graph can be considered infinite and regular."

This is false, and it is the classic trap. On a k-Cayley tree the boundary fraction tends to (k−2)/(k−1), a finite fraction — 2/3 for k = 3. That is exactly why the Cayley tree and the Bethe lattice are not thermodynamically equivalent, and why the Bethe–Peierls result must be understood as the deep-bulk / locally-tree-like result rather than as "the exact solution of the Cayley tree". Rewrite as: "the boundary retains a finite weight, so the Bethe–Peierls solution describes the bulk of a locally tree-like graph (e.g. a random regular graph), not the Cayley tree's thermodynamics." Your own Fig. 15 middle panel (random regular graph, where BP works perfectly) makes exactly this point.

2.8 Proof A.4 is invalid for bipartite graphs

The step "Since |λ_k| < ρ for all k ≥ 2…" fails precisely for connected bipartite graphs, where −ρ is also an eigenvalue. Since the square and simple-cubic lattices — bipartite, and central to Chapter 5 — are the main examples, this is worth fixing. The conclusion h(G) = ln ρ(A) still holds; you need irreducible + aperiodic (primitive) for the argument as written, or a one-line remark handling the periodic case. Also Eq. (141) assumes A is diagonalisable, and Eq. (142) should read W_n ∼ Cρⁿ, not lim W_n = Cρⁿ.

2.9 "Non-bipartite" is an unnecessary hypothesis for uniqueness

The box "Uniqueness of the stationary distribution" (§3.1) and Theorem A.2 both assume *"connected, non-bipartite"*. The proof in A.2 never uses non-bipartiteness — and correctly so: the stationary distribution is unique for any connected undirected graph. Non-bipartiteness (aperiodicity) is needed for convergence, not uniqueness — which the very next paragraph then explains via the lazy walk. Drop the hypothesis from the theorem, and keep the aperiodicity discussion where it is. Same remark for the Kac's-lemma box.

3. Numerical / quantitative inconsistencies (Chapter 5 and around)

# Where Issue
3.1 p. 28 "an effective non-zero critical threshold p_c ∼ 1/ln N ≈ 0.08". 1/ln(10⁴) = 0.109, not 0.08. (0.08 is the right answer via p_c = ⟨k⟩/(⟨k²⟩−⟨k⟩) with ⟨k²⟩ ≈ 55 for BA at N = 10⁴ — so give that calculation instead of the 1/ln N shortcut.) 3.2 p. 35–36 The square lattice is described as being "on a torus", but the reported λ₂ says otherwise. For a 25×25 periodic grid, λ₂ = 2(1−cos 2π/25) = 0.0629. The thesis reports λ₂ = 0.0158, which is exactly 2(1−cos π/25) — i.e. open boundary conditions. Same in 3D: λ₂ = 0.1522 = 2(1−cos π/8) → an 8×8×8 = 512-node open cube, not the "N ≈ 600–1000" stated. 3.3 p. 39 *"Euclidean topologies relax with characteristic times τrel = 1/(2λ₂) scaling as N^{−2/3}"*. Backwards: λ₂ ∼ N^{−2/3} in 3D, so **τrel ∼ N^{+2/3}** — relaxation gets slower with size. The Conclusions (§6.1 point 2) state it correctly as λ₂ ∼ N^{−2/d}; §5.4 does not. Also, N is never varied, so no scaling is actually demonstrated — either run 2–3 sizes or phrase it as an expectation.
3.4 p. 40, §6.2 Two percentage errors computed with different denominators. 2D: (2.89−2.27)/2.27 = 27%. 3D: (4.93−4.51)/4.93 = 8.5%. Consistently normalised by the exact value, 3D gives 9.3%; normalised by BP, 2D gives 21.5%. The "27% → 8.5%" improvement is therefore partly an artefact of the convention. 3.5 p. 40 "tetrahedral loop echoes in Pyrochlore lower T_c by ∼15% relative to the unfrustrated Simple Cubic". −15% is exact-vs-exact (3.80 vs 4.51); the simulated values give 4.20 vs 4.50 = −6.7%. 3.6 Table (122) BA row: "T_c^exact = ∞". The simulated network is finite (N ≈ 1000), where T_c ≈ 13.8 by your own mean-field column. ∞ is a thermodynamic-limit statement placed in a column of values for finite simulated systems. Write → ∞ (N → ∞) in a footnote, not as a table entry.
3.7 p. 40, §6.2 *"The BA network retains finite magnetization (⟨|m|⟩ ≈ 0.35 at T = 6.5) without a susceptibility peak, confirming the infinite critical temperature."* It confirms T_c > 6.5, nothing more — and the expected finite-N value is ≈ 13.8, which is simply outside the plotted range [1, 6]. Extend the temperature range to ~15 (cheap) and the claim becomes a real, quantitative test of DGM. As it stands the figure cannot show what the caption says. Same for Fig. 15's right panel (N = 400, range 1–6).
3.8 Fig. 24 µ = 1.48 ± 0.07 is called "the universal critical transport exponent" (§6.1 point 4). One system size, one realisation set, no finite-size scaling, pc read off a finite-system susceptibility peak (which is biased), and a fit over well under a decade in (p−pc) — with the fitted exponent highly sensitive to the assumed pc, whose uncertainty is not propagated. Also, µ(λ₂) = 1.48 and µ(flux) = 0.96 are two different exponents for two different observables, yet the Conclusions quote only 1.48 as "the" transport exponent. Downgrade to "an effective exponent µ ≈ 1.5 for this system size", drop "universal", and compare with the known 3D conductivity exponent t ≈ 2.0 for random resistor networks. Likewise, pc ≈ 0.53 for site percolation on a-Si is never compared with the diamond-lattice literature value (0.43) — a one-line comparison would strengthen the section considerably.
3.9 Fig. 16 vs Fig. 19 The 2D benchmark measures "shortest ring" per node; the 3D benchmark measures "chordless rings". These are different quantities and the 2D and 3D distributions are therefore not comparable — yet they are presented in parallel and compared in the Conclusions. Use one definition throughout, or state the difference explicitly.
3.10 Fig. 19(a) / Fig. 21(a) Panel header reads "3D Simple Cubic Lattice, z = 6, Bravais (100% 4-rings)" while the plotted distribution in the same figure is P(4) = 0.50, P(6) = 0.50. Direct contradiction inside one figure (a leftover from the 2D version of the panel).
3.11 p. 36 vs Fig. 16 Text: *"P(7) ≈ 18.7%"* for MAC; figure: 18.5%. Trivial, but they should match.
3.12 Fig. 14 vs Fig. 18 The 2D square Ising transition is simulated twice (N = 1600 and N = 900) with inconsistent conclusions: Fig. 14 says the transition occurs "exactly at Onsager's critical temperature", Fig. 18 reports a peak at T_c^sim ≈ 2.35 (+3.5%). 3.13 Chapter 5 throughout No simulation protocol is given anywhere: number of MC sweeps, equilibration/burn-in, measurement interval, number of independent runs per temperature, random seeds, software. For 2D RGG and MAC even the sample construction (r_c, box size, periodicity) is only partly stated. Add a short "Numerical methods" subsection at the start of Chapter 5 — this is the single cheapest way to raise the chapter's credibility, and an examiner will ask.

5. Terminology and notation

# Issue
5.1 γ carries three different meanings: degree-distribution exponent (Eq. 30), percolation susceptibility exponent (Eq. 86) — in the same section — and spectral gap (Eq. 60). Rename at least two of them (e.g. spectral gap → g or Δ).
5.2 H carries three meanings: Shannon entropy H(X), mean first-passage time H(i,j), heat kernel H_t — plus the Hamiltonian H(σ). Four, actually.
5.3 λ and k: λ(G) is edge connectivity (Eq. 19) while λ_i are eigenvalues; k(G) is node connectivity while k_i is degree. Use κ(G) and λ_e(G), as is standard.
5.4 Walk vs. path. Eqs. (12)–(14) call [A^r]_ij "the number of paths of length r" and Tr(A^r) "the total number of cycles of length r". Both count walks (repeated nodes allowed, including backtracking i→j→i), and Tr(A^r) overcounts each genuine cycle by 2r for undirected graphs. Since §4.3 later builds an entire subsection on precisely the difference between backtracking and non-backtracking walks, getting this right in Chapter 2 matters. Eq. (76) has the same issue ("admissible paths" → walks).
5.5 Entropy units are inconsistent: Eqs. (73)–(75) use log₂ (bits), Eqs. (77)–(82) use ln (nats). h_walk and h(G) are never directly compared, but a reader will try. Pick one base.
5.6 Eq. (26): the sum condition j ≠ k ≠ i does not exclude j = i; and betweenness is usually normalised and summed over unordered pairs. State the convention.
5.7 Eq. (24)/(25): PageRank needs 0 < α < 1 for (I − αW) to be invertible; the text only says α, β > 0. Also the standard choice β = (1−α)/n is worth stating.
5.8 §2.5: *"the uniform unit vector (1,1,…)"* — that vector is not a unit vector. Trivial, but it is a definition box.
5.9 §2.3: ⟨d⟩ is called "typical correlation length". It is a mean geodesic distance; correlation length is a different object (and, on networks, genuinely subtle). Drop the parenthetical.
5.10 Eq. (43) vs Eq. (65): two different processes are both called "diffusion" — dp/dt = −LD⁻¹p (random walk, stationary state k_i/2m) and df/dt = −Lf (heat, stationary state uniform). The thesis never points out that they have different equilibria, which is one of the most instructive facts in this whole area. A two-line remark would add real value.
5.11 Eq. (59)/(60): the ordering is written 1 = λ₁ > \|λ₂\| ≥ … but the spectral gap is then defined as γ = 1 − λ₂ rather than 1 − \|λ₂\|. Inconsistent (and the distinction is exactly the bipartite case discussed two paragraphs earlier).
5.12 Table 1: "Regular lattice, ⟨d⟩ = O(n)" — true for a 1D ring only; the text one page earlier correctly says N^{1/d}. Also ER's C = p is better written O(1/n) to make the → 0 explicit, and "Hub: rare" for Watts–Strogatz is generous — WS has no hubs at all (its degree distribution is narrower than Poisson).

6. Historical / bibliographic

# Issue
6.1 §1.1: "models proposed by Van der Waals and later Ernst Ising demonstrated … spontaneous symmetry breaking and phase transitions." Ising demonstrated the opposite. The 1925 paper solves the 1D chain, finds no transition, and Ising incorrectly generalised that to higher dimensions. The transition was established by Peierls (1936) and solved by Onsager (1944). As written this is a factual error in the opening page.
6.2 §1.1: *"In 1998, Watts and Strogatz discovered the small-world phenomenon in natural networks."* The phenomenon is Milgram (1967); WS introduced the model that reconciles short paths with high clustering.
6.3 §1.2: "a complex system is typically one where the Mean-Field approximation fails." By that definition the 2D square-lattice Ising model is a complex system — and Chapter 5 spends a figure showing MF failing on exactly that lattice. The definition undercuts the thesis' own framing. Either qualify it (heterogeneity + fluctuations + no characteristic scale) or drop it.
6.4 Missing citations for results actually used: Shannon (1948) for Eq. (73) — [Jay57] is Jaynes on maximum entropy, not the definition of Shannon entropy; Metropolis et al. (1953) and Hastings (1970) for the algorithm (currently only [Bin81]); Erdős–Rényi (1959/60) for G(N,p); Kac for Kac's lemma; Cohen–Erez–ben-Avraham–Havlin (2000) for p_c → 0 on scale-free networks (§4.1 attributes it implicitly to Molloy–Reed); Sykes–Essam for the honeycomb thresholds; the 3D Ising Tc ≈ 4.5115 (Ferrenberg et al.); the exact kagomé Tc ≈ 2.1433; the pyrochlore MC T_c ≈ 3.8; Clauset–Shalizi–Newman for power-law fitting (see 4.2); and source citations for the two empirical networks in Fig. 6.
6.5 No data/code availability statement. For a thesis whose second half is entirely computational, a one-line pointer to a repository (or to an appendix listing the scripts) is worth a lot.
